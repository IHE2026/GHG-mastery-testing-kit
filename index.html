Foundation 🟢 Beginner · Lesson 1 of 14 F-01

What Are
Greenhouse Gases?

Before counting emissions, you must understand what you're counting — the science, the gases, and why businesses are legally and financially required to measure them.

📌 Learning Objectives
  • Name the 7 greenhouse gases covered by the GHG Protocol
  • Explain the greenhouse effect and its link to climate change
  • Understand why businesses measure and publicly report emissions
  • Define CO₂e and understand why it exists

The Greenhouse Effect — In Plain English

Earth receives solar energy. Some reflects back into space. Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere trap a portion of that outgoing energy — like a blanket around the planet. This natural effect keeps Earth habitable. The problem: human activity is thickening that blanket far faster than at any point in measurable history.

The main human driver is burning fossil fuels: coal, oil, and natural gas. When burned, they release CO₂ and other gases that have been locked underground for millions of years. Add to that: deforestation, industrial processes, agriculture, and waste — and you have a warming trend that is measurably disrupting global weather, sea levels, and ecosystems.

Key Definition
Greenhouse Gas (GHG)
A gas that absorbs and re-emits infrared radiation, trapping heat in the lower atmosphere. The GHG Protocol covers seven gases, each with a different capacity to warm the planet. All are converted to a single common unit — CO₂e — for reporting and comparison.

The 7 Gases You Must Know

The GHG Protocol Corporate Standard requires reporting companies to account for all seven gases listed under the Kyoto Protocol. Each has a different warming power:

GasSymbolPrimary SourceGWP₁₀₀ (AR5)Why It Matters
Carbon DioxideCO₂Fossil fuels, deforestation1Baseline — all gases measured against this
MethaneCH₄Livestock, landfill, oil & gas28–36Short-lived but far more potent than CO₂
Nitrous OxideN₂OAgriculture, wastewater, combustion265–298Long atmospheric lifetime; huge in farming
HydrofluorocarbonsHFCsRefrigerants, aerosolsup to 14,800Replaced CFCs but still very potent
PerfluorocarbonsPFCsAluminium smelting, semiconductors7,390–12,200Extremely long-lived in atmosphere
Sulphur HexafluorideSF₆Electrical switchgear23,500Highest GWP of the seven gases
Nitrogen TrifluorideNF₃Electronics manufacturing16,100Added in Kyoto 2nd commitment period
💡
Exam Tip: CO₂, CH₄, and N₂O represent over 90% of most corporate inventories. Prioritise these three. HFCs matter significantly in cold chain, retail, and pharmaceutical sectors where refrigerants are in heavy use.

Why Do Businesses Report GHGs?

🏢
Real Driver: Regulation + Finance
UK SECR · EU CSRD · SEC Climate Disclosure · TCFD
UK companies with 250+ employees or £36M+ turnover must report Scope 1 and 2 under Streamlined Energy and Carbon Reporting (SECR). The EU's Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) from 2024 mandates Scope 3 for ~50,000 companies. Institutional investors and lenders increasingly require verified GHG data before committing capital — making this a financial risk issue, not just an environmental one.

What Is CO₂e?

Different gases trap different amounts of heat. To compare and aggregate them meaningfully, we convert all gases to a common unit: CO₂ equivalent (CO₂e). This uses each gas's Global Warming Potential (GWP) over 100 years.

Conversion Formula — Used for Every Gas
CO₂e = Quantity of gas × GWP₁₀₀
Example 1: 1 tonne CH₄ × GWP 28 = 28 tonnes CO₂e
Example 2: 1 tonne SF₆ × GWP 23,500 = 23,500 tonnes CO₂e
Example 3: 1 tonne N₂O × GWP 265 = 265 tonnes CO₂e

The GHG Protocol

The world's most widely-used standard for corporate GHG accounting — what it is, who made it, its five core principles, and how it defines the three scopes.

📌 Learning Objectives
  • Explain what the GHG Protocol is and who governs it
  • State the five principles of high-quality GHG reporting
  • Define organisational boundary using the two GHG Protocol approaches
  • Explain the difference between Scope 1, 2, and 3 at a conceptual level

What Is the GHG Protocol?

The GHG Protocol is a suite of accounting standards developed by the World Resources Institute (WRI) and the World Business Council for Sustainable Development (WBCSD). First published in 2001, it is now used by more than 90% of Fortune 500 companies that report emissions — making it the de facto global standard.

The Primary Standard
Corporate Accounting and Reporting Standard
The foundational GHG Protocol document. It defines how companies measure, manage, and disclose their GHG emissions across Scope 1, 2, and 3. This is what the CSRWorks certification pathway is built on.

The Five Reporting Principles

Every GHG inventory must be built on these five principles. Verifiers and auditors test inventories against them:

PrincipleWhat It RequiresPractical Failure Example
RelevanceReport all emission sources material to your inventoryA logistics company omitting its fleet fuel use
CompletenessInclude all sources within your chosen boundaryIncluding diesel fleet but only some of the vehicles
ConsistencyUse the same methods across all periods for comparabilitySwitching emission factor tables mid-year without restating base year
TransparencyDisclose all assumptions, methodologies, and data sourcesReporting a final number with no footnote on which GWP AR was used
AccuracyMinimise bias and uncertainty; use best available dataEstimating gas consumption from bills instead of reading meters

Setting Organisational Boundaries

Before counting a single tonne of CO₂, you must define: what is your organisation? The GHG Protocol offers two approaches:

🏗️
Equity Share Approach
Report in proportion to your financial ownership stake
Account for emissions proportional to your equity share. If you own 40% of a joint venture, report 40% of its emissions — regardless of who manages it. Common in investment-heavy industries and financial reporting contexts.
⚙️
Control Approach (Most Common)
Report 100% of emissions from operations you control
Report 100% of emissions from any operation over which you have operational or financial control. If you control how a facility is run — even if you only own 51% — 100% of its emissions are yours. This is the most widely adopted approach under SECR, CSRD, and TCFD frameworks.
⚠️
Common Mistake: Organisations with joint ventures, outsourced logistics, or leased buildings frequently apply boundaries inconsistently. Document your approach once and apply it every year — switching boundary methods requires restating all prior years.

GWP & the Core Calculation

Master the fundamental calculation that underpins every GHG inventory ever produced — from converting single gas quantities to building full multi-source emission totals.

📌 Learning Objectives
  • Explain GWP₁₀₀ and why IPCC AR4 and AR5 give different values
  • Apply the three-part emission calculation formula to any source
  • Convert any gas quantity to CO₂e correctly
  • Calculate a total CO₂e from a mixed multi-source scenario

GWP₁₀₀ — The 100-Year Standard

GWP measures how much heat the emission of 1 tonne of a gas absorbs relative to 1 tonne of CO₂ — over a 100-year window. The "100" is the standard for corporate reporting. A shorter window (GWP₂₀) makes methane look far more dangerous; the 100-year view moderates this. Always state which horizon you are using.

📘
AR4 vs AR5: IPCC Assessment Report 5 (2014) updated GWP values. CH₄ moved from 25 (AR4) to 28 (AR5). Most current reporting frameworks accept both but require you to declare which you used and apply it consistently throughout the inventory.
Master Formula — Used in Every GHG Inventory
Emissions (CO₂e) = Activity Data × Emission Factor × GWP
Activity Data = what was consumed or produced (litres of fuel, kWh of electricity, tonnes of waste)
Emission Factor (EF) = gas released per unit of activity (e.g. kg CO₂ per litre of diesel)
GWP = converts gas mass to CO₂e (CO₂ = 1, CH₄ = 28, N₂O = 265 per AR5)

Worked Example — Diesel Fleet Vehicle

🚛
Step-by-Step: Three-Gas Calculation
UK diesel delivery truck · DEFRA 2023 emission factors
Given: 10,000 litres diesel consumed in a year.
DEFRA EFs: CO₂ = 2.68 kg/litre · CH₄ = 0.00024 kg/litre · N₂O = 0.000042 kg/litre

CO₂: 10,000 × 2.68 × 1 (GWP) = 26,800 kg CO₂e
CH₄: 10,000 × 0.00024 × 28 = 67.2 kg CO₂e
N₂O: 10,000 × 0.000042 × 265 = 111.3 kg CO₂e

Total: 26,978.5 kg = 26.98 tCO₂e
Note: CO₂ dominates (99.3%). CH₄ and N₂O are small here — but not always.

What Is Scope 1?

Direct emissions from sources owned or controlled by your organisation — the four categories, the boundary test, and how to distinguish Scope 1 from everything else.

📌 Learning Objectives
  • Define Scope 1 and pass the boundary test for any emission source
  • Classify sources into the four Scope 1 sub-categories
  • Distinguish Scope 1 from Scope 2 and 3 with worked examples
  • Apply real industry examples including agriculture and manufacturing
GHG Protocol — Scope 1 Definition
Direct GHG Emissions
Emissions from sources that are owned or controlled by the reporting organisation. The physical release of greenhouse gas occurs within your operational boundary — in equipment, vehicles, or processes that you own or operate.

The Four Source Categories

CategoryDescriptionReal Examples
Stationary CombustionBurning fuel in fixed equipment at a facilityGas boilers, diesel generators, industrial furnaces, incinerators
Mobile CombustionBurning fuel in vehicles owned by the companyCompany cars, HGVs, forklifts, owned aircraft, own ships
Process EmissionsChemical or physical reactions during productionCement calcination (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂); steel blast furnace; fertiliser manufacture
Fugitive EmissionsUnintended leaks or venting from owned equipmentHFC leaks from own refrigeration; CH₄ from coal mines; SF₆ from owned switchgear
🔑
The Three-Question Boundary Test: (1) Does the company own the physical asset? (2) Does it control how the asset is operated? (3) Does the emission happen inside that asset? If YES to all three → Scope 1.

Real Industry Examples

🐓
Poultry Farm, Imo State Nigeria
3,000-bird layer operation — Scope 1 identification
Scope 1 sources:
• Diesel generator combustion (stationary)
• CH₄ and N₂O from manure management (process/fugitive)
• N₂O from land application of manure
• LPG combustion for on-site feed processing

NOT Scope 1: Grid electricity for lighting → Scope 2. Third-party feed production → Scope 3 Cat 1. Worker travel to farm → Scope 3 Cat 7.
🏭
UK Food Manufacturer
Three production sites + refrigerated fleet
Scope 1: Natural gas boilers · company-owned refrigerated trucks · HFC top-ups in cold storage · on-site backup diesel generator.

Rough annual total: 440 tCO₂e (gas) + 127 tCO₂e (trucks) + 333 tCO₂e (HFC leaks) = 900 tCO₂e Scope 1

The HFC leaks (333 tCO₂e) from just 85 kg of refrigerant nearly match the entire gas boiler footprint. Never underestimate high-GWP fugitives.

Scope 1 Emission Factors

Emission factors are the conversion rates that transform litres, cubic metres, and tonnes into tCO₂e. Knowing where to find them, how to select them, and when to use defaults versus national data is a core professional skill.

📌 Learning Objectives

  • Identify authoritative databases: DEFRA, IPCC, EPA, Ecoinvent

Definition
Emission Factor (EF)
A coefficient that converts a unit of activity into a mass of greenhouse gas. Emission factors are derived from combustion chemistry, measured laboratory data, and real-world monitoring — then published by government bodies and international organisations.

Scope 2 · Purchased Energy 🟡 Intermediate · Lesson 7 of 14 S2-01

What Is Scope 2?

Indirect emissions from electricity, heat, steam, or cooling that is purchased from outside the organisation and consumed within it. The emissions occur at the power plant — but the GHG Protocol assigns responsibility to the buyer.

📌 Learning Objectives
  • Define Scope 2 and identify its four energy types
  • Explain the "indirect" logic — why buyer responsibility applies
  • Distinguish Scope 2 from Scope 1 for the same energy type
  • Identify Scope 2 sources across different sectors
GHG Protocol — Scope 2 Definition
Indirect Emissions from Purchased Energy
Emissions from the generation of electricity, heat, steam, or cooling that is purchased and consumed by the reporting organisation. The physical release of CO₂ happens at the generation facility — not at your building — but you are the economic demand that made it necessary.

The Four Scope 2 Energy Types

TypeCommon ExamplesTypical Sectors
Purchased ElectricityGrid power for lighting, HVAC, machinery, computersAll sectors — the most universal Scope 2 source
Purchased HeatDistrict heating hot water from a centralised networkOffice buildings, residential, hospitals
Purchased SteamIndustrial process steam supplied by a third partyHeavy industry, food processing
Purchased CoolingChilled water from a district cooling systemData centres, hot climates, large commercial buildings
💡
Critical Distinction: If you generate your own electricity using a diesel generator → Scope 1 (combustion in your owned equipment). If you buy electricity from the national grid → Scope 2. The classification depends on who owns the generator, not the type of energy consumed.

Scope 2 · Purchased Energy 🟠 Advanced · Lesson 8 of 14 S2-02

Market-Based vs
Location-Based

The 2015 Scope 2 Guidance mandated two accounting methods — both required in every report. Most companies get this wrong. This lesson explains the difference, when each applies, and how RECs, GOs, and PPAs affect your numbers.

📌 Learning Objectives
  • Define and distinguish the Market-Based and Location-Based methods
  • Explain what RECs, GOs, and PPAs are and how they change Market-Based Scope 2
  • Understand dual reporting requirements under the 2015 Guidance
  • Calculate both figures and present them correctly
Method 1
Location-Based
Uses the average emission intensity of the electricity grid in the geographic area where consumption occurs. Answers: "What is the average CO₂ per kWh delivered to users on this grid?" Uses published grid average emission factors from IEA, EPA eGRID, or national bodies. Reflects physical reality.
Method 2
Market-Based
Uses the emission factor of the specific electricity product the company has contracted to purchase. A Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) with a wind farm, or a Renewable Energy Certificate (REC/GO), can support a zero emission factor claim. Reflects contractual choices and market signals.
TechCo UK — Both Methods Compared
5,000 MWh consumed · 100% REGO renewable tariff
Worked Example
Location-Based
5,000,000 kWh × 0.2120 kg/kWh ÷ 1000 = 1,060 tCO₂e
Grid average for UK 2023. What TechCo would emit if the grid mix applied to it.
Market-Based (with REGOs)
5,000,000 kWh × 0 kg/kWh = 0 tCO₂e
TechCo holds REGOs for 100% of consumption. Market-Based = 0. This is what companies mean by "100% renewable electricity."
⚠️
The Debate: Market-Based = 0 sounds clean, but the physical electrons on the shared grid are still mostly fossil-generated. Location-Based tells the physical truth. This is why both figures must be reported and labelled clearly — reporting only zero would be misleading without context.

Calculating Scope 2

Step-by-step calculations using both methods — Location-Based and Market-Based — with a multi-country worked example including UK and Nigeria operations.

📌 Learning Objectives

  • Apply the Market-Based formula with supplier instruments
  • Handle multi-country operations with different grid intensities
  • Present both Scope 2 figures correctly in a GHG inventory table
Scope 2 — Both Methods
Location-Based (tCO₂e) = kWh × Grid Avg EF ÷ 1,000
Market-Based (tCO₂e) = kWh × Supplier/Instrument EF ÷ 1,000
Where no instrument exists → use residual mix factor (≥ national grid average EF)
AgroCarbon Group — Multi-Country Scope 2
UK: 1,200 MWh · Nigeria: 450 MWh
Worked Example

Location-Based: 1,200,000 × 0.2120 ÷ 1,000 = 254.4 tCO₂e
Market-Based: No instrument → use residual mix (0.2120) = 254.4 tCO₂e

Nigeria site (450 MWh, IEA grid EF = 0.3946 kg/kWh):

Scope 3 · Value Chain 🟠 Advanced · Lesson 10 of 14 S3-01

What Is Scope 3?

All other indirect emissions in the value chain — upstream and downstream. For most companies this represents 70–95% of their total GHG footprint, and it is the hardest, most demanded skill in the ESG profession.

📌 Learning Objectives
  • Define Scope 3 and explain why it dominates most corporate GHG footprints
  • Distinguish upstream from downstream Scope 3
  • Understand materiality screening and why the 80/20 rule applies
  • Explain which Scope 3 categories are mandatory vs optional
GHG Protocol — Scope 3 Definition
Indirect Emissions from the Value Chain
All other indirect GHG emissions that occur in a company's value chain — both upstream (before inputs reach you) and downstream (after products leave you). These occur outside your operational control but within your sphere of influence as buyer, producer, or investor.

Scope 3 Data Methods

Four calculation methods for Scope 3 — from spend-based screening to supplier-specific primary data. Knowing which to use, and when to upgrade, is what separates a practitioner from a generalist.

📌 Learning Objectives
  • Distinguish four calculation methods by accuracy, data demand, and appropriate use case
  • Apply the spend-based method for first-inventory screening
  • Understand the 80/20 rule for prioritising data collection effort
  • Explain the supplier engagement journey from generic to primary data
MethodData RequiredAccuracyBest Applied To
Spend-BasedSpend (£) × economic intensity EF (kg CO₂e/£)Low — order of magnitude onlyFirst inventory screening; categories with no physical data
Average-DataPhysical quantity × industry-average EF (Ecoinvent, DEFRA)MediumCategories where quantities are known but supplier data is unavailable
Supplier-SpecificActual verified emissions data from the supplier (product carbon footprint)HighHigh-spend, high-impact Cat 1 suppliers with mature MRV
Activity-BasedFull process-level data — energy, materials, transport per stepHighest (most expensive)Capital goods, Cat 11 use-phase, LCA-level work
📘
The 80/20 Rule in Scope 3: 80% of emissions come from 20% of spend categories. Start with spend-based to identify your hotspots. Invest data collection only in the top 3–5 categories. Trying to collect primary data for all 15 categories simultaneously in Year 1 is a common and costly mistake.
🌾
Spend-Based Screening in Practice
Category 1 — Purchased Agricultural Inputs
Company spends £200,000 on steel components.
Spend-based EF for steel: 1.538 kg CO₂e per £ (derived from industry revenue vs emissions data).
Estimated Cat 1: £200,000 × 1.538 = 307,600 kg = 307.6 tCO₂e

Limitation: If steel prices spike, the same physical quantity appears more carbon-intensive. Use this figure to prioritise steel as a hotspot, then switch to average-data (tonnes of steel × kg CO₂e/tonne) for the next reporting cycle.
